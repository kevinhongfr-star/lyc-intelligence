## 1. Model Explanation

SPARK is a 27-item self-assessment that measures a leader's AI readiness across three interconnected dimensions: **Individual AI Adoption Readiness** (D1), **Capability Exposure Assessment** (D2), and **Organisational Preparedness** (D3). Each dimension contains 9 Likert-scale items (1–5), normalised to a 0–20 scale. The composite score is the average of the three dimensions, expressed as a 0–100 percentile.

The model's core insight is that AI readiness is not purely a technology problem — it is a leadership problem operating at three levels simultaneously:

1. **The individual level** — whether the leader personally uses, evaluates, and integrates AI tools
2. **The awareness level** — whether the leader understands what AI can do in their domain and what it means for their capabilities
3. **The organisational level** — whether the structures, policies, and data infrastructure exist to support systematic AI deployment

A leader can be strong at one level and weak at another, and the archetype system captures these combinations.

### Why Three Dimensions, Not Two

The engine config originally used a 2×2 archetype matrix with axes labelled "board_ai_fluency" and "governance_maturity" — implying a board-specific assessment. However, the question bank clearly measures a broader leadership population, with items about personal workflow, domain capability awareness, and general organisational policy. The canonical model retains the 2×2 archetype structure but reframes it as **Individual AI Fluency** (combining D1 + D2 signal) versus **Organisational Governance Maturity** (D3 signal), which aligns with the actual item content.

The third dimension (D2 — Capability Exposure Assessment) is reported separately because awareness of AI's domain-specific capabilities is a distinct capability from hands-on tool use. A leader can use AI tools daily (high D1) without a clear picture of what's changing in their broader professional domain (variable D2), and vice versa.

## 2. Dimension Interrelationships

### D1 × D2: The Fluency-Awareness Gap
A common pattern: leaders who use AI tools in their own work (high D1) but have not systematically assessed what AI means for their broader professional capabilities (mixed D2). Conversely, some leaders research AI extensively and understand the landscape (high D2) but have not integrated tools into their daily workflow (low D1). Both patterns create blind spots.

- **High D1 + High D2**: The leader is genuinely AI-fluent — using tools daily *and* understanding the broader capability shift. This is the foundation of the AI Champion archetype.
- **High D1 + Low D2**: The leader is a tool user but not a strategic thinker about AI. They may be automating yesterday's workflows without understanding tomorrow's capability shifts.
- **Low D1 + High D2**: The leader understands the AI landscape conceptually but has not built hands-on fluency. Their awareness outpaces their capability to act.
- **Low D1 + Low D2**: The leader is neither using AI nor tracking its development. This is the Unprepared Leader quadrant.

### D2 × D3: The Strategy-Governance Gap
Capability awareness (D2) and organisational preparedness (D3) often move together but can diverge. A leader in a well-governed organisation may have low personal awareness, relying on policy to compensate. A leader with strong personal awareness may work in an organisation with no AI governance structure.

- **High D2 + High D3**: Strategic awareness backed by institutional structure. The leader understands the landscape *and* has the organisational support to act.
- **High D2 + Low D3**: The leader sees what's coming but lacks the organisational structure to respond. This is a source of frustration that can lead to either advocacy or disengagement.
- **Low D2 + High D3**: The organisation has policies and structure, but the leader does not personally understand AI capabilities well enough to make informed decisions within that structure.
- **Low D2 + Low D3**: Neither individual awareness nor institutional structure exists. Systemic risk is high.

### D1 × D3: The Adoption-Governance Gap
Individual tool adoption and organisational governance maturity can also diverge. Shadow AI (high D1 in a low-D3 environment) creates quality and compliance risk. Over-governance (high D3 with low D1 adoption) can stifle innovation.

## 3. Archetype Detail

### A1 — AI Champion
**Matrix Quadrant:** High Individual Fluency × High Governance Maturity  
**Typical Profile:** Senior leader in a technology-forward organisation who personally uses AI tools, understands the capability landscape in their domain, and operates within a mature governance environment.

**Core Pattern:**  
The AI Champion sees AI as a strategic lever for organisational performance, not just a personal productivity tool. They advocate for structured AI adoption, can speak credibly about both capability and governance, and push their teams and peers to build fluency.

**Strengths:**
- Personal credibility — they use AI themselves, so their advocacy is not theoretical
- Systems thinking — they understand both the capability opportunity and the governance requirements
- Change orientation — they actively look for workflows to improve and are willing to restructure established practices

**Growth Areas:**
- **Pacing risk:** Champions often move faster than their organisations can absorb, creating friction and resistance
- **Overconfidence bias:** High personal fluency can lead to underestimating how much learning others need
- **Governance impatience:** They may see policies as barriers rather than enabling structures

**Development Focus:**
- Build coalition across readiness levels — find early adopters in each function
- Translate AI capability into business outcomes that non-technical stakeholders care about
- Develop structured change management approach rather than relying on enthusiasm alone

---

### A2 — Skeptical Expert
**Matrix Quadrant:** High Individual Fluency × Low Governance Maturity  
**Typical Profile:** A technically proficient leader who uses AI tools personally and understands their capabilities and limitations well, but works in an organisation with weak or no AI governance.

**Core Pattern:**  
The Skeptical Expert knows more about AI than most people in their organisation, and this knowledge makes them cautious. They have seen AI produce incorrect or low-quality output, and they are skeptical that their organisation can manage AI well. Their skepticism is rooted in genuine expertise, not resistance to technology.

**Strengths:**
- Deep, realistic understanding of what AI can and cannot do in their domain
- Quality orientation — they will not deploy AI solutions that degrade output quality
- Identifies genuine risks that enthusiasts may miss

**Growth Areas:**
- **Blocking vs. building:** Skepticism can become a default posture that slows all AI adoption, not just bad AI adoption
- **Disengagement risk:** When organisational policy lags behind capability, experts may check out of the AI conversation entirely
- **Framing:** They may frame concerns as quality issues when the real need is governance infrastructure

**Development Focus:**
- Channel critical thinking into building governance structures — become the person who designs AI quality policy, not just the person who points out flaws
- Separate "this specific use case is not ready" from "AI is not ready"
- Identify high-confidence, low-risk use cases where AI value is clear and governance is straightforward

---

### A3 — Policy-First Adopter
**Matrix Quadrant:** Low Individual Fluency × High Governance Maturity  
**Typical Profile:** A leader who works in an organisation with formal AI policies, tool approvals, and review processes, but has not built deep personal fluency with AI tools themselves.

**Core Pattern:**  
The Policy-First Adopter operates within a well-structured AI environment and trusts the organisation's governance. They may approve AI initiatives, oversee teams using AI, or sit on AI governance committees — but their personal hands-on experience is limited. They rely on policy and expert review rather than their own judgment about AI output quality.

**Strengths:**
- Works within a safe, compliant AI environment
- Understands the importance of governance and review processes
- Lower risk of ungoverned or reckless AI use

**Growth Areas:**
- **Governance without understanding:** They may approve or oversee AI work without being able to evaluate its quality independently
- **Process dependency:** Over-reliance on review processes can slow innovation and reduce the leader's ability to spot genuine issues
- **Peer disadvantage:** Leaders who combine fluency with structure will make better decisions faster

**Development Focus:**
- Build hands-on AI fluency in their specific professional domain — start with 2–3 tools relevant to their work
- Learn to evaluate AI output quality directly, not just through policy lenses
- Develop personal workflow integration to understand AI's practical implications from the inside
- Use the organisation's governance structure as a safety net while building personal capability

---

### A4 — Unprepared Leader
**Matrix Quadrant:** Low Individual Fluency × Low Governance Maturity  
**Typical Profile:** A leader who has not yet engaged with AI at a personal level and works in an organisation with little or no formal AI governance structure.

**Core Pattern:**  
The Unprepared Leader is operating in a pre-AI leadership mode. They may be aware that AI exists, but they have not integrated it into their work, have not assessed what it means for their domain, and do not have organisational structures to rely on. The gap is both individual and institutional.

**Strengths:**
- Awareness of the gap is the first step — and growth trajectory can be steep
- No bad habits from ungoverned experimentation to unlearn
- Opportunity to build capability the right way from the start

**Growth Areas:**
- **Structural disadvantage:** AI-ready peers and competitors will outpace them on speed, quality, and cost
- **Decision risk:** Poor decisions about AI investment, hiring, and policy due to limited understanding
- **Team impact:** Their team's AI capability will reflect their own lack of engagement

**Development Focus:**
- Start with foundational AI literacy — what AI tools exist in their domain, what they do well, what they do poorly
- Pick one high-value workflow and build hands-on experience with a single tool
- Simultaneously push for basic governance — tool approval policy, output review standards
- Address individual and organisational levels in parallel — one without the other limits progress

## 4. Interpretation Framework

### Composite Score Interpretation

| Band | Score Range | What It Means | Action Orientation |
|------|-------------|---------------|-------------------|
| AI-Ready Organisation | 80–100 | Strong across all three dimensions. Systematically capturing AI gains. | Expand scope, mentor others, advance to next-level AI integration. |
| Building AI Capability | 60–79 | Solid foundation with gaps in one or two dimensions. | Target the lowest dimension with specific development investment. |
| Early AI Adoption | 40–59 | Foundational capability developing but not systemic. | Prioritise 1–2 dimensions for focused attention; build before scaling. |
| AI Capability Gap | 0–39 | Significant gaps across all dimensions. | Urgent investment required; start with foundational literacy and basic governance. |

### Dimension-Level Interpretation

Each dimension is scored 0–20 (normalised) with three verdict levels:

- **Strong (16–20):** Mature capability with demonstrated organisational impact. This dimension is a strength and can serve as a foundation for further development.
- **Developing (10–15.9):** Emerging capability with targeted development opportunities. The leader has some capability but it is not yet consistent or systematic.
- **Gap (0–9.9):** Significant capability gap requiring immediate attention. This dimension is a risk area that will increasingly affect performance.

### Dimension Interaction Patterns to Watch For

1. **D1 High, D3 Low (Shadow AI Pattern):** The leader uses AI tools extensively but without organisational oversight. Risk: quality inconsistencies, data privacy issues, compliance exposure. Action: advocate for basic governance policies while maintaining adoption momentum.

2. **D3 High, D1 Low (Policy Without Fluency):** The organisation has strong governance but the leader has low personal fluency. Risk: they approve or oversee AI work they cannot evaluate. Action: build personal fluency while leveraging the governance structure as a safety net.

3. **D2 Low Across the Board (Awareness Blind Spot):** Low D2 combined with any D1/D3 profile means the leader is not tracking AI's evolving capabilities in their domain. Even high adopters can become obsolete if they stop tracking the broader landscape. Action: schedule regular capability landscape reviews.

4. **All Three Low (Systemic Gap):** Low scores across all three dimensions indicate a leader who is genuinely behind on AI readiness, and this gap will compound. Action: immediate, structured intervention at both individual and organisational levels.

## 5. Edge Cases and Nuances

### Reverse-Coded Item Impact
Each dimension contains exactly 3 reverse-coded items (33% of items). This is a robust psychometric design that reduces response bias. However, it also means that participants who rush through the assessment without reading carefully will have inconsistent response patterns that artificially lower their scores. The normalised scoring partially mitigates this, but practitioners should note that very low scores may partly reflect careless responding rather than genuine low readiness.

### The 2×2 Matrix With 3 Dimensions
SPARK reports three dimensions but uses a 2×2 archetype matrix. This is not an inconsistency — it reflects the model's psychometric structure: D1 and D2 both load onto the broader "individual AI fluency" factor, while D3 represents the distinct "organisational governance" factor. The 2×2 captures the primary classification, while the three-dimension profile provides diagnostic granularity.

Practitioners should avoid saying "SPARK is a 2×2 model with four archetypes" as if that were the full picture. The accurate statement is: "SPARK measures AI readiness across three dimensions, and classifies leaders into four archetypes based on the interaction between individual fluency and organisational governance."

### Organisational Level vs. Individual Perception
D3 (Organisational Preparedness) is measured through the leader's self-report, not through organisational audit. A leader in a well-governed organisation may score D3 low if they are unaware of existing policies and structures. Conversely, a leader in a poorly governed organisation may overestimate governance maturity if they are not close to policy decisions.

Interpretation note: D3 scores reflect the leader's *perception* of organisational preparedness, which is what matters for their behaviour and decision-making, but may not perfectly match objective organisational reality.

### Domain-Specific Variability
AI capability varies dramatically by professional domain — a lawyer faces different AI disruption than a consultant, who faces different disruption than a financial analyst. SPARK items are domain-general enough to work across professional services, but the *interpretation* of scores should be calibrated to the leader's specific domain. A score of 65 in a domain where AI is still nascent may be relatively strong, while the same score in a rapidly AI-transforming domain may be a laggard position.
